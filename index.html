Diabetic Retinopathy Classification

Completed

National AI Competition 2026 · Top 6 finalist, Computing Track · Mar to Jun 2026

A deep learning system that grades the severity of diabetic retinopathy from retinal images, built by our four-member team, Tony Stork. I worked on dataset cleaning, research, coding, and experiments with MaxViT and ConvNeXt models. Our ensemble reached 80.4% validation accuracy.

Project brief, from our competition poster
Problem
Diabetic retinopathy is a condition where high blood sugar damages the delicate blood vessels in the eye. Its effects range from blurry vision to permanent loss of sight.
Our solution
An AI model that predicts which stage of diabetic retinopathy a person has from an image of their retina. It gives the predicted stage, its confidence as a percentage, and the reasoning behind the decision.
Model
An ensemble: several MaxViT and ConvNeXt models each make a prediction, and a majority vote decides the final answer.
Data preparation
Ben Graham preprocessing and resizing, then augmentation with random rotation, horizontal flips, brightness and contrast changes, and YOCO. A weighted sampler balances the classes during training.
Why an ensemble
It reduces individual model errors and the bias from a severely imbalanced dataset, captures more varied visual features, generalises better, and gives more confident predictions.
Limitations
The model does not know the situation behind a case, such as age, gender, or genetic conditions. It can make mistakes, so it cannot give a final clinical decision.
Responsibilities
Do not overstate how effective the AI is. Train only on data that does not infringe on personal information without permission. Maintain, secure, and update the model regularly.

Team Tony Stork: Samuel, Jun Lum, Srijith and me, mentored by Ms Nadiah Zainal Abidin.

VentPal

Prototype

Xplore AI Hackathon, Hyperbyte.ai · 2025

A conversational AI concept from a two-day hackathon: an affordable way for young people to talk through their problems, by simulated video or audio call. Gemini writes each reply, ElevenLabs turns it into speech, and an avatar speaks it. I worked on the idea, the feature plan, and presenting it.

Project brief, from our hackathon poster
Problem
Many young people lack the resources to get mental health support. Therapy can be expensive, the wait to see someone is long, and it is hard to find a person they trust. General chatbots are an option, but talking to a cold AI does not feel right.
Objective
Give young people an affordable way to share their problems anytime and anywhere.
Solution
An AI chatbot that can be customised to give each person the most suitable support.
Advantages
An interface that simulates a video call with a real person. Individualised, unbiased responses. It recognises how severe a problem is and connects the user to a real professional or a call for real help.
Our plans
Build it as a web platform and launch it inside Sunway University's network first. Then survey users for feedback and improve it.
Ethical consideration
Users choose whether their conversation is recorded. For those who allow it, the conversations are kept properly protected.

Built with my teammates Hao Wee, Suen and Afiqah.

Why I'm building Fair Hire

A teacher once told our class something I couldn't stop thinking about: one reason women and men are treated unequally at work starts before the interview, with the photo on a résumé.

Read the full post

A photo says nothing about whether someone can do the job. But it's often one of the first things a recruiter sees, and it can quietly shape who gets shortlisted and who doesn't.

For a class project on UN Sustainable Development Goal 5, Gender Equality, my group had to pitch a solution. We pitched Fair Hire: an AI tool that helps recruiters focus on what actually shows ability, like skills, experience and results, instead of details that don't.

I didn't want it to stay a slide in a pitch deck. So now I'm building it for real, as an AI agent for the Create AI Agents competition by Dubai Chamber.

The hard part isn't hiding a photo. Bias can sneak in through many small signals, and AI can learn bias too if you're not careful. Building something fair means testing it, questioning it, and being honest about what it can't do.

I'll write more here as it takes shape.

Thank you to my teammates Aurora Firdaus and Lee Choeun (Eila), who pitched Fair Hire with me, and to my teacher, Ms Lois Abigail Harper, whose lesson sparked the idea and who supported us all the way.

Losing NAIC 2025 was the start of my Top 6

In 2025 I entered the National AI Competition, and I lost. A year later my team made the Top 6. This is about the year in between.

Read the full post

Losing NAIC 2025 hurt. I had put real effort in, and it still wasn't enough. I could have taken that as a sign that AI competitions weren't for me.

Instead I treated it as unfinished business. NAIC 2026 was going to be my redemption.

This time I came back with a team of four, Tony Stork (Samuel, Jun Lum, Srijith and me), and a problem that matters: diabetic retinopathy, a condition where high blood sugar damages the blood vessels in the eye. If it's caught late, it can mean permanent loss of sight. We built a model that grades how severe it is from an image of the retina.

My part was cleaning the dataset, research, coding, and running experiments with MaxViT and ConvNeXt models. We combined them into an ensemble that reached 80.4% validation accuracy.

We made the Top 6 in the Computing Track, and I received an education bursary with it.

The result felt good. But what I'm proudest of is coming back at all. Losing once didn't end anything. It just showed me where I needed to grow.

Thank you to my teammates Samuel, Jun Lum and Srijith, and to our mentor, Ms Nadiah Zainal Abidin, for guiding us through it.

The hackathon we didn't know was coming

Our first hackathon ever, and we didn't even know it was coming. Then the judges' questions nearly stumped us.

Read the full post

In 2025 I joined the Xplore AI programme by Hyperbyte.ai expecting workshops and learning. Then, at the end, came the surprise: a hackathon. Build something, and pitch it. My team was Hao Wee, Suen, Afiqah and me.

It was our first hackathon ever. Honestly, we had no idea what to do. No plan, no idea, and not much time.

So we started from a problem instead of a technology. A lot of young people don't get mental health support. Therapy can be expensive, the waits are long, and it's hard to find someone you trust. Typing your feelings into a general chatbot doesn't feel the same as talking to someone.

That became VentPal: an AI you can talk to through a simulated video or audio call. Gemini writes the reply, ElevenLabs gives it a voice, and an avatar says it out loud. It was designed to notice when a problem is serious and point the person to real help.

Then came the pitch. The judges asked really hard questions, the kind we hadn't prepared for, and for a moment we were stuck. But we worked through it together and managed to defend our idea.

It was only a concept and a prototype, but it taught me two things I still use. You don't need a perfect plan to start: pick a real problem, and build toward it. And when you get a question you can't answer right away, don't panic. Think it through out loud, and defend what you believe in.

Thank you to my teammates Hao Wee, Suen and Afiqah, who figured it out with me.
