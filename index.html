Diabetic Retinopathy Classification

Completed

National AI Competition 2026 · Top 6 finalist, Computing Track · Mar to Jun 2026

A deep learning system that grades the severity of diabetic retinopathy from retinal images, built by our four-member team, Tony Stork. Our ensemble of MaxViT and ConvNeXt models reached 80.4% validation accuracy.

Project brief, from our competition poster
Problem
Diabetic retinopathy is a condition where high blood sugar damages the delicate blood vessels in the eye. Its effects range from blurry vision to permanent loss of sight.
Our solution
An AI model that predicts which stage of diabetic retinopathy a person has from an image of their retina. It gives the predicted stage, its confidence as a percentage, and the reasoning behind the decision.
Model
An ensemble: several MaxViT and ConvNeXt models each make a prediction, and a majority vote decides the final answer.
Data preparation
Ben Graham preprocessing and resizing, then augmentation with random rotation, horizontal flips, brightness and contrast changes, and YOCO. A weighted sampler balances the classes during training.
Why an ensemble
It reduces individual model errors and the bias from a severely imbalanced dataset, captures more varied visual features, generalises better, and gives more confident predictions.
Limitations
The model does not know the situation behind a case, such as age, gender, or genetic conditions. It can make mistakes, so it cannot give a final clinical decision.
Responsibilities
Do not overstate how effective the AI is. Train only on data that does not infringe on personal information without permission. Maintain, secure, and update the model regularly.

Team Tony Stork: Samuel, Jun Lum, Srijith and me. Mentored by Ms Nadiah Zainal Abidin, with guidance from Mohith and Ian.

FoodSaver

Prototype

National AI Competition 2025 · AI Innovation Track · Team 404 Brain Not Found

An app to stop good food from being thrown away. Malaysians waste thousands of tonnes of food every day, much of it bought too much and then forgotten. VentPal

Prototype

Xplore AI Hackathon, Hyperbyte.ai · 2025

A conversational AI concept from a two-day hackathon: an affordable way for young people to talk through their problems, by simulated video or audio call. Gemini writes each reply, ElevenLabs turns it into speech, and an avatar speaks it. I worked on the idea, the feature plan, and presenting it.

Project brief, from our hackathon poster
Problem
Many young people lack the resources to get mental health support. Therapy can be expensive, the wait to see someone is long, and it is hard to find a person they trust. General chatbots are an option, but talking to a cold AI does not feel right.
Objective
Give young people an affordable way to share their problems anytime and anywhere.
Solution
An AI chatbot that can be customised to give each person the most suitable support.
Advantages
An interface that simulates a video call with a real person. Individualised, unbiased responses. It recognises how severe a problem is and connects the user to a real professional or a call for real help.
Our plans
Build it as a web platform and launch it inside Sunway University's network first. Then survey users for feedback and improve it.
Ethical consideration
Users choose whether their conversation is recorded. For those who allow it, the conversations are kept properly protected.

Built with my teammates Hao Wee, Suen and Afiqah.

How a holiday camp at 12 led me to AI

I didn't start with AI. I started with wires, a soldering iron, and a school holiday my mum didn't want me to waste.

Read the full post

When I was 12, my mum sent me to a robotics and electronics camp at the German-Malaysian Institute (GMI) during the school holidays, together with my neighbours. We got to play with a robotic hand and explore electric cars, back when almost nobody in Malaysia had even seen an EV. That was the first time I saw that you could make things move and react just by connecting the right parts.

After the camp, I bought my own Arduino and started building my own little projects. I honestly don't remember what they were anymore, but I remember how it felt to make something work.

Then COVID hit, and Malaysia went into the Movement Control Order. At 13, stuck at home, I wrote my first code and built my first website, using Swift. I loved it. (That website is lost forever now.) At 14, still in lockdown, my sister told me to learn Python. Best advice she's ever given me.

At 15, for my final project in the IB Middle Years Programme, I built an automatic rubbish bin that opens its lid by itself. It became a hit in my batch, and my teacher showcased it to all the juniors. That was the first time something I built was shown to other people.

At 19, in college, AI still wasn't part of my life. Most of us barely used it. Then the Hyperbyte.ai team came to Sunway College for Xplore AI, with Kai Song, Jay Lim and Zad Chin teaching us. That's where I trained my first object detection model, and where a surprise hackathon turned into VentPal.

Then I lost NAIC 2025. And honestly, I thought about quitting AI and putting it all behind me. It felt like it wasn't my thing.

But I didn't want to waste the skills I'd spent years building, all the way back to that camp at 12. So I kept going: NAIC 2026, where my team made the Top 6, then MYSSP, Fair Hire for Dubai Chamber, and now the Young Founders Summit.

Along the way, I started studying economics, and it opened my mind. I began to see how many problems in the world still haven't been solved: poverty, inequality, fraud, waste. That's why I care about where AI meets economics and public policy. I don't just want to build cool things anymore. I want to build things that help solve those problems.

Thank you to my mum for that first camp, to my sister for telling me to learn Python, and to Kai Song, Jay Lim and Zad Chin for my first real steps in AI.

Why I'm building Fair Hire

A teacher once told our class something I couldn't stop thinking about: one reason women and men are treated unequally at work starts before the interview, with the photo on a résumé.

Read the full post

A photo says nothing about whether someone can do the job. But it's often one of the first things a recruiter sees, and it can quietly shape who gets shortlisted and who doesn't.

For a class project on UN Sustainable Development Goal 5, Gender Equality, my group had to pitch a solution. We pitched Fair Hire: an AI tool that helps recruiters focus on what actually shows ability, like skills, experience and results, instead of details that don't.

I didn't want it to stay a slide in a pitch deck. So now I'm building it for real, as an AI agent for the Create AI Agents competition by Dubai Chamber.

The hard part isn't hiding a photo. Bias can sneak in through many small signals, and AI can learn bias too if you're not careful. Building something fair means testing it, questioning it, and being honest about what it can't do.

I'll write more here as it takes shape.

Thank you to my teammates Aurora Firdaus and Lee Choeun (Eila), who pitched Fair Hire with me, and to my teacher, Ms Lois Abigail Harper, whose lesson sparked the idea and who supported us all the way.

Losing NAIC 2025 was the start of my Top 6

In 2025 I entered the National AI Competition, and I lost. A year later my team made the Top 6. This is about the year in between.

Read the full post

NAIC 2025 was my first national AI competition. My team, 404 Brain Not Found, built FoodSaver: an app that tracks the expiry dates of the food you store, reminds you before it goes bad, and uses AI to turn what's left in your fridge into a recipe. The goal was less food waste, tied to UN Sustainable Development Goals 1 and 2, No Poverty and Zero Hunger.

It was hard. We were all busy with our own commitments, and honestly, a lot of the project came together at the last minute. We didn't win.

Losing hurt, and I knew why: an idea isn't enough if you don't give it the time it needs. I could have taken that as a sign that AI competitions weren't for me.

Instead I treated it as unfinished business. NAIC 2026 was going to be my redemption.

This time I started early and came back with a team of four, Tony Stork (Samuel, Jun Lum, Srijith and me), and a problem that matters: diabetic retinopathy, a condition where high blood sugar damages the blood vessels in the eye. If it's caught late, it can mean permanent loss of sight. We built a model that grades how severe it is from an image of the retina.

We made the Top 6 in the Computing Track, and I received an education bursary with it.

The result felt good. But what I'm proudest of is coming back at all. Losing once didn't end anything. It just showed me where I needed to grow.

Thank you to my 2025 team, Nikita, Nyip Sian and Sin Yee, and our mentor Ms Deepa, for my first try. And to my 2026 teammates Samuel, Jun Lum and Srijith, to our mentor, Ms Nadiah Zainal Abidin, and to Mohith and Ian, who guided us throughout our NAIC journey.

The hackathon we didn't know was coming

Our first hackathon ever, and we didn't even know it was coming. Then the judges' questions nearly stumped us.

Read the full post

In 2025 I joined the Xplore AI programme by Hyperbyte.ai expecting workshops and learning. Then, at the end, came the surprise: a hackathon. Build something, and pitch it. My team was Hao Wee, Suen, Afiqah and me.

It was our first hackathon ever. Honestly, we had no idea what to do. No plan, no idea, and not much time.

So we started from a problem instead of a technology. A lot of young people don't get mental health support. Therapy can be expensive, the waits are long, and it's hard to find someone you trust. Typing your feelings into a general chatbot doesn't feel the same as talking to someone.

That became VentPal: an AI you can talk to through a simulated video or audio call. Gemini writes the reply, ElevenLabs gives it a voice, and an avatar says it out loud. It was designed to notice when a problem is serious and point the person to real help.

Then came the pitch. The judges asked really hard questions, the kind we hadn't prepared for, and for a moment we were stuck. But we worked through it together and managed to defend our idea.

It was only a concept and a prototype, but it taught me two things I still use. You don't need a perfect plan to start: pick a real problem, and build toward it. And when you get a question you can't answer right away, don't panic. Think it through out loud, and defend what you believe in.

Thank you to my teammates Hao Wee, Suen and Afiqah, who figured it out with me.

Where Silence Breathes

My instructor tore the mask from my face without any warning. Thirteen meters down, inside a flooded cave, in total darkness. This is the story of my cavern training at Sipadan.

Read the full story

My instructor tore the mask from my face without any warning. Salt water flooded into my eyes as darkness surrounded me. Instinctively, I reached forward, searching for the guideline laid inside the cave, while my flashlight carved a weak beam through the flooded dark ahead. My breathing grew louder than my thoughts. Inhale slowly, exhale slowly, one breath at a time. I was somewhere inside the Turtle Tomb Cave, more than thirteen meters from the surface, where panic could become fatal.

The cave was unlike any darkness I had known before. It was not the darkness of night, nor the darkness of shutting your eyes tight, covering them with both hands and pulling a blanket over your head. It was a darkness so complete that you lose your sense of direction, distance and even time itself. The only other light came from my dive computer and my glow-in-the-dark SPG, which didn't help at all. The cave began with a wide entrance, where faint traces of natural light streamed through the water. But the deeper we swam, the narrower the chambers became. Sharp rocks closed around us while silt drifted through the water like flies, threatening to drop our visibility to zero. Every moment had to stay controlled. A single careless mistake could damage equipment, waste air or trap a diver in the confined passageways.

On our way out, my SPG got caught in a sharp, V-shaped rock, and I had to move backwards to free myself. It took quite some time, and I kept worrying about my instructor's air and my own, since we had been inside for about an hour. After I finally untangled it, I zoned out for a while, staring at the entrance, when a large red fish with glowing red eyes suddenly appeared in my flashlight beam. It startled me so much that my regulator almost slipped from my mouth. I thought nothing lived inside the cave. Despite the stress, I forced myself to slow down. Inside a cave, panic only makes everything worse. Around me, there was no sound except our breathing, bubbles escaping upward, and the faint clicking of metal equipment against stone.

Only a few days earlier, I had arrived at Sipadan to complete my cavern diving and nitrox certifications. I stayed at the Seaventures Dive Rig, the world's first dive rig in the middle of the ocean. Unlike ordinary dive centres on beaches or private islands, Seaventures felt cut off from the rest of the world. Supplies, luggage and even visitors were lifted up on a large wooden platform suspended above the sea. Parts of the structure had no proper safety railings, so one careless step could send someone falling 8 meters into the ocean below, and you had to bring your passport just in case a strong current swept you to Indonesia or the Philippines. The rig itself looked like a floating metal city stranded in endless water.

Life there quickly settled into a strange rhythm. Every morning, I woke up to the sound of waves crashing against the pillars beneath my room while sunlight reflected across the clear blue water. Fish could easily be seen from the surface, swimming around the rig as if they never slept. Every day, brief generator blackouts swallowed the rig in darkness before the lights slowly returned. At night there was only the howling wind, the crashing waves, scattered stars above us and endless sea in every direction. Removed from traffic, crowded streets and daily routines, my time on the rig slowly stopped feeling real.

My first dive began shortly after I arrived. After the equipment check and lunch, we descended into the water below the rig for an evaluation dive. Even before reaching Sipadan, the ocean already felt different from anywhere I had dived before. Then came The Drop Off, a world-renowned wall dive just meters from the shore that plunges over 600 meters until it disappears into darkness. Looking into those depths filled me with awe and fear. Part of me jokingly imagined a megalodon hiding somewhere below, waiting for the right moment to have us for breakfast. That's why I stayed close to the reef wall on certain dives. If anything came up from beneath us, I figured my friends would probably get eaten first, giving me enough time to escape.

Despite those thoughts, Sipadan was breathtaking. Looking around felt like staring into another world entirely. Schools of barracuda twisted around us like a silver tornado, and a shiver of sharks moved silently past. Sea turtles drifted calmly through the current, including one carrying a visible tumour on its body. Moray eels hid inside the coral, and one almost attacked me, while stingrays glided silently across the sand. At one point, a pufferfish suddenly inflated itself into a floating ball, and everyone laughed through their regulators. Sipadan kept shifting between beauty and danger. One moment felt peaceful, and the next reminded me how fragile humans truly are beneath the ocean.

Yet despite the danger, the friendships became one of the most unforgettable parts of the experience. Although we came from different backgrounds and countries, the divers on the rig quickly became family, and so did the staff. The chefs became like mothers to me, asking which dishes I wanted for dinner. Underwater, we constantly checked on one another, especially in strong currents. But once we surfaced, everything turned back into jokes and laughter. Most evenings ended with stories, gossip and teasing around the dinner table. Strangers quickly became a temporary family, and as the youngest diver on the rig at the time, I felt truly loved.

Still, nothing compared to the pressure of cavern training. It was the most technical and mentally exhausting diving I had ever done. Unlike open water, there is no direct path to the surface in an emergency, and every movement inside the cave matters. My instructor constantly simulated the worst possible emergencies to prepare me. Sometimes he flooded my mask completely, or blindfolded me, forcing me to navigate in total darkness using only touch. We also practised broken torches, running out of air and zero visibility. On our first cave dive, our main line became tangled, which is a bad sign, and we wasted valuable time and air struggling to free it. At the same time, my instructor's regulator began malfunctioning, releasing white, cloudy bursts of air. I still remember staring blankly at him like a five-year-old who had made a mistake, trying to understand what was happening while silently trusting him to stay calm.

The deeper we travelled into the cave, the quieter everything became. No engines, no voices, no distractions. Only our breathing. On my earlier dives, my heart rate stayed above one hundred beats per minute from stress and adrenaline. But over time, the darkness that once terrified me began to feel strangely peaceful. My breathing slowed. My heartbeat dropped closer to its normal resting rate. Somewhere inside the flooded cave, surrounded by silence and darkness, I discovered a calmness I couldn't find anywhere else.

Ironically, some of the funniest moments came right after the most stressful dives. At dinner, my instructor dramatically complained about his regulator incident to another divemaster while everyone sat around the table. The divemaster laughed and said my training dive had suddenly turned into a rescue mission. Then I joked that if my instructor had died inside the Turtle Tomb Cave, I would have laid him beside the turtle skeletons and renamed it "Chye Tomb Cave". The whole table burst into such hysterical laughter that we were all crying. Sitting in the middle of the ocean, surrounded by darkness, joking about death after a dangerous dive somehow felt normal. Divers understand fear differently, and humour becomes part of how we survive.

Even life outside the dives felt disconnected from reality. The internet signal on the rig was extremely weak, and after I had disappeared underwater for long stretches, my mother panicked and called the dive centre because I hadn't replied to her messages. While she imagined the worst happening somewhere beneath the sea, I was underwater, laughing with other divers and singing Christmas songs. The outside world slowly faded away.

Long after leaving Sipadan, I still remember every incident, every story and every bit of gossip we shared, especially the laughter around the dinner table. I remember every morning boat ride, all of us struggling to keep our eyes open. Most of all, I remember the silence. The Turtle Tomb Cave is filled with the remains of animals that were trapped inside, a reminder of how unforgiving nature can be. It taught me that darkness does not always mean defeat. As long as the will to survive remains, you can keep moving forward. Fear and peace can exist in the same place, and sometimes the quietest places reveal the clearest parts of ourselves. Beneath the ocean, I found a kind of peace I couldn't find anywhere else.
